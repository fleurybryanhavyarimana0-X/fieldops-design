Design case study · Portfolio project, 2026

FieldOps: one alert, two people, two screens.

A dispatcher dashboard and an in-cab driver app for yard and fleet operations, built on one shared design system. I framed the problem, designed both surfaces, and built them as working front-end code on top of my own telemetry pipeline.

The problem

Telemetry systems are good at producing alerts and bad at getting them handled. On the industrial sites I worked with, supervisors received long lists of events with no order, and drivers only heard about a problem from a radio call after the fact. The same event, an engine idling for ten minutes, meant very different things to the two people involved.

Dispatcher

  • Watches 20 to 60 assets across a shift
  • Needs to know what to act on first, not everything that happened
  • Works at a desk with two screens, often on the phone

Driver / operator

  • In a moving, vibrating cab, often wearing gloves
  • Glances at the screen for one or two seconds
  • Wants context for an alert and a way to explain it ("I'm waiting to load")

How I framed it

I treated each alert as a small workflow with an owner rather than a notification. The question for the dispatcher became "what needs a decision now?" and for the driver "what is the one thing to do?".

DetectThe pipeline validates and de-duplicates events, then raises speeding, idle and overheat alerts.
Tell the driverA plain-language nudge in the cab, with one large response button.
Queue for dispatchCritical first, then newest; each row names the asset, what happened and when.
Close the loopAcknowledge, or see the driver's reason, so the same alert is not chased twice.

Key decisions

Status is never colour alone.Each status pill pairs a word with a shape: a circle for on track, a diamond for warning, a triangle for critical. It stays readable for colour-blind users and on washed-out cab screens in sunlight.
Two target sizes, one system.Desk controls are 36 px tall; cab controls are 56 px with larger type, because gloves and vibration make small targets fail. Both come from the same tokens.
The driver can answer the alert."I'm waiting to load" pauses the idle alert and tells dispatch why. It turns a blame signal into information and cuts the radio calls that follow every idle alert.
Summary before detail.The dispatch board opens on four numbers and an ordered queue. The fleet table and asset detail are there when needed, not before.

Design system

Both surfaces use one token file (colour, type, space, target sizes) and one component sheet. Dark mode is defined at the token level, so every component works in both themes.

  • Text colour pairs chosen for WCAG AA contrast in light and dark themes
  • Visible focus ring on every control; motion turned off for users who ask for reduced motion
  • Tabular numerals wherever figures line up in columns

What I would test next

  • Ride-alongs with drivers to measure glance time on the nudge and adjust type size and wording
  • A week of dispatch shadowing to tune the queue order, for example whether repeat idling should outrank a single overheat
  • Whether "I'm waiting to load" reduces radio calls about idling, compared with a week without it
